Output of the 2026 Forum · Leipzig · 2–5 June 2026

Position Paper

This position paper gathers the demands that emerged from the Erasmus+ Future Forum. Written and voted by participants, it is now open for associations and networks to sign before being sent to the European Commission.

Key demands

The participants of the Erasmus+ Future Forum call on the European Commission, EACEA, the European Parliament, the Council and the National Agencies to:

On the holistic dimension of skills

  • Embed labour-market skills as a horizontal priority across all Erasmus+ objectives, so employability complements but never overrides inclusion, citizenship and intercultural learning.
  • Align the Union of Skills with the EU's own competence frameworks (GreenComp, DigComp, EntreComp, LifeComp).
  • Ring-fence at least 25% of the Erasmus+ Youth budget for inclusion, participation, intercultural dialogue and community resilience.
  • Introduce a dedicated quality label for youth-work organisations.

On the European Solidarity Corps

  • Maintain the ESC as a standalone programme, separate from Erasmus+, with its own objectives, governance and visibility.
  • Define clear complementarities with Erasmus+ while safeguarding each mission.
  • Introduce common digital tools and a single entry point to simplify access.
  • Strengthen quality assurance, monitoring and impact assessment across both programmes.

On the recognition of non-formal learning

  • Strengthen the recognition of Youthpass in education and the labour market, integrated with Europass and clear validation pathways.
  • Improve quality and standardisation of Youthpass implementation, with competence-based descriptions and a participant feedback cycle.
  • Develop a flexible, multi-level recognition system across youth exchanges, volunteering, training and VET, including digital badges.
  • Embed Youthpass as an integral, participatory part of project design.

On budget, distribution and inclusion

  • Increase the overall budget to at least €60 billion (towards €80 billion with Agora EU), indexed for inflation across the full period.
  • Distribute funds more fairly, with higher recognition for Erasmus+ Youth and earmarks for vulnerable sectors.
  • Treat third-country inclusion as added value, without diverting the budget for EU countries.
  • Address global challenges promptly; efficiency gains from AI must not justify a smaller budget.

Beyond employability: the holistic dimension of skills

The gap

The Union of Skills agenda frames skills primarily as a driver of competitiveness and employability — a pipeline from education to the labour market. But the competences Erasmus+ has long nurtured — intercultural understanding, civic participation, belonging, community-oriented thinking — rarely translate into immediate labour-market deliverables, even though they build cohesive, inclusive societies. An employability-first framing risks upskilling part of the workforce while leaving others behind, particularly newcomers from outside the EU, and quietly narrowing which organisations can access the programme. Because priorities decide who can realistically apply, grassroots and arts-based organisations working on social, cultural, democratic and community issues risk being pushed to the margins — and the young people who most depend on non-linear pathways (NEETs, migrants, rural youth) are the least well served.

The evidence

The 2025 Erasmus+ interim evaluation finds that organisations with non-formal mandates increasingly struggle to demonstrate labour-market impact, with consequences for Key Action 2. The European Youth Work Agenda and the Council Resolution on the youth field 2019–2027 recognise quality youth work as a distinct field beyond employability; the European Youth Forum's "Skills Beyond Jobs" warns that a narrow definition marginalises non-formal pathways, and UNESCO treats civic and intercultural competences as foundational. SALTO-YOUTH data show most Erasmus+ Youth projects target inclusion, participation or intercultural dialogue — exactly what a competitiveness-first frame would de-prioritise.

What we ask, and why it is feasible

We ask that labour-market skills be a horizontal priority across all objectives rather than a standalone pillar, aligned with the EU's own frameworks (GreenComp, DigComp, EntreComp, LifeComp); that at least 25% of the Erasmus+ Youth budget be ring-fenced for inclusion, participation, intercultural dialogue and community resilience; and that a dedicated quality label recognise youth-work organisations. This is realistic: inclusion and active citizenship are already objectives of Regulation (EU) 2021/817, LifeComp shows the appetite for non-labour-market tools, and several National Agencies have piloted youth-work standards. Left unaddressed, the programme risks becoming a vocational supplement rather than a transformative educational tool.

The new Erasmus+ must build a Union of Skills for everyone, not a pipeline for the few — employability matters, but so do solidarity, intercultural understanding and active citizenship.

Beyond skills: a standalone European Solidarity Corps

The gap

The Commission's 2028–2034 proposal would fold the European Solidarity Corps into a future Erasmus+ under the Union of Skills. Presented as simplification, it would in fact shift how the EU approaches youth engagement. Since its creation the ESC has given young people a distinct way to contribute through volunteering and solidarity, building cohesion, active citizenship and European belonging. A skills-oriented umbrella risks eroding its visibility, autonomy and mission — and downgrading solidarity as a value that exists beyond employability. The first to feel it would be young people and the small civil-society organisations built around the ESC's mission, and the communities their projects serve.

The evidence

The Commission's own ESC evaluation highlights the added value of the programme's dedicated quality standards, including the ESC Quality Label, and locates the real challenge not in its separate existence but in the need for stronger synergies and better tools — flagging IT problems and reporting delays — while noting funding closer to a pilot than to its ambitions. The ACA's analysis of the 2028–2034 proposal shows impact, inclusion and innovation are achievable through coordinated, complementary programmes without merger; the European Youth Forum's "Don't Cut Young People Out" argues the ESC should stay autonomous. Practice already proves it: Erasmus+ and the ESC share National Agency structures in many Member States while keeping separate objectives and identities.

What we ask, and why it is feasible

We support a common digital entry point and better coordination — but visibility must not cost the ESC its identity. We ask the Commission to preserve the ESC as a distinct programme with its own objectives, governance and visibility; to define clear complementarities with Erasmus+; to introduce shared digital tools to simplify access; and to strengthen quality assurance across both. Synergy does not require merger — shared National Agencies already show coordination without consolidation, and a visible solidarity programme serves the Commission's own priorities of simplification, inclusion and cohesion.

Simplify access through shared tools and coordination — not through merging programmes with different objectives. Keep both visible, distinct and fit for purpose.

From participation to recognition: non-formal learning

The gap

Non-formal education gives young people real skills and experiences that formal systems and employers still overlook: the learning happens, but it is neither visible nor validated. Those most affected are young people, youth workers and the organisations whose outcomes go unrecognised — to employers, universities and the learners themselves.

The evidence

The EU has already committed to this. The 2012 Council Recommendation on validating non-formal and informal learning says such competences enhance employability, mobility and lifelong learning — especially for the disadvantaged — and calls on Member States to involve youth organisations in designing validation. Yet delivery is uneven: Cedefop's 2023 inventory shows the issue is everywhere on the agenda but not yet delivered, and the Commission's own evaluation found validation incomplete and asymmetric. The value is not "soft" — a study for the European Youth Forum found five of the six soft skills employers most want are built through youth organisations, and research shows volunteering builds measurable "employability capital." The tool already exists: Youthpass is the recognition instrument for Erasmus+ Youth and the ESC, and its reach is real — 71% of ESC participants would not have found a similar opportunity elsewhere, with young people with fewer opportunities rising from 25% (2018) to 40% (2023).

What we ask, and why it is feasible

We ask that Youthpass recognition be strengthened in education and the labour market and integrated with Europass; that its implementation be standardised with competence-based descriptions and a feedback cycle; that a flexible, multi-level recognition system span exchanges, volunteering, training and VET, using digital badges; and that Youthpass be embedded as a participatory part of project design, not an add-on. The precedent is firmly set — Articles 165–166 TFEU, the 2012 Recommendation, the 2022 micro-credentials Recommendation, the European Year of Skills and the EU Youth Strategy all point the same way. The instruments exist; what is needed is to name, resource and quality-assure them in the next Regulation.

From participation to recognition: the recognition of non-formal education must be standardised alongside formal education within Erasmus+.

A budget that matches the ambition

The gap

The next programme would succeed both Erasmus+ and the ESC with an indicative budget of about €36.2 billion in 2025 prices (€40.8 billion in 2026 prices). Inflation over seven years, new responsibilities and rising allowances risk leaving it short of demand. Three problems compound this: sectoral distribution is unclear, putting smaller sectors at risk; the basis for including non-programme (third) countries is not yet convincing; and global challenges — including AI for tasks such as translation — must be handled properly, never used to justify cuts. An under-funded or opaque programme creates unequal access, hitting young people hardest, since they already face the tightest loop of limited opportunity.

The evidence

The European Parliament's EPRS analyses of the 2028–2034 programme and budget, and the European University Association's call for a properly funded Erasmus+, both warn the proposed envelope is not commensurate with the programme's own targets once inflation and the integration of the ESC are accounted for.

What we ask, and why it is feasible

We ask that the overall budget rise to at least €60 billion — towards €80 billion if Agora EU or other programmes are folded in — indexed for inflation across the full period; that funds be distributed more fairly, with higher recognition for Erasmus+ Youth and earmarks for vulnerable sectors; that third-country inclusion be treated as added value rather than a diversion of EU-country budget; and that efficiency gains from new technology never become a pretext for cuts. The "Youth in Action" programme is a precedent for a holistic, youth-centred approach; echoing the BUDG position, we call for clear, separate budget lines per pillar, sector and instrument, with reporting that shows real community impact.

The Erasmus+ budget must rise to €60 billion to guarantee equal access for all beneficiaries and real impact in the communities the programme serves.
