What the 2027 Guide tells us about the future of Erasmus+

The last Guide of the current programme already points towards 2028–2034. We read it line by line against the 2026 edition, through the four demands of our Collective Position Paper.

20 November 2026·6 min read

Annual Programme Guides rarely make headlines. They are long, technical and mostly unchanged from one year to the next. The 2027 edition, published on 18 November, deserves a closer look: it is the last Guide under the 2021–2027 Regulation, it re-anchors the programme on the Education and Skills Package the Commission adopted on 5 November 2026, and it cites the proposal for the next Erasmus+ (COM(2025) 549) more than once.

In other words, it is the closest thing we have to a preview of the priorities that will shape 2028–2034. We compared it with the 2026 Guide and read the differences through the four demands of the Collective Position Paper.

Through the lens of the four demands

01 · Budget Rates frozen

A final year without an increase

The overall envelope for 2021–2027 is unchanged at “more than 26 billion EUR” (p.20), and every unit cost and lump sum we checked is identical to 2026: €71 a day of individual support in a Youth Exchange in Germany (p.164), €680 for a preparatory visit, lump sums of €120,000, €250,000 or €400,000 for Cooperation Partnerships and €30,000 or €60,000 for Small-scale Partnerships.

Rates that stay flat while costs rise are exactly why the Paper asks for a budget of at least €60 billion, indexed for inflation, in 2028–2034.

02 · Skills Rewritten

Basic skills and employability move to the front

The first youth priority for partnerships keeps its title but not its content. In 2026 it put the inclusion of young people with fewer opportunities “at the core”. In 2027 it is about key competences and basic skills “as identified in the Education and Skills Package”, with informal learning aimed at employability (p.232). Inclusion remains a horizontal priority of the whole programme, but this is the shift the Paper warns about: skills framed first by the labour market.

There is good news too. Citizenship now counts as a basic skill (p.11), and Youth Participation Activities open with “Youth participation is a fundamental right” (p.179).

03 · Solidarity Corps On the record

The merger is already cited

A footnote in the reaccreditation chapter (p.44) cites the proposal for the next Erasmus+ as the act “repealing Regulations (EU) 2021/817 and (EU) 2021/888”. The second is the European Solidarity Corps Regulation.

The Guide does not cover the Solidarity Corps, which has its own guide, but the citation shows how the transition is being prepared. The Paper asks for the Corps to remain a programme with its own objectives, governance and visibility.

04 · Recognition A step forward

Europass and Youthpass enter the award criteria

Both partnership formats now list Europass and Youthpass among the tools projects should use (p.240, p.249). In 2026 the Small-scale Partnerships list named neither. Part A also says EU tools underpin the recognition of learning acquired through mobility (p.14).

A welcome step. The Paper asks for more: that non-formal learning in youth work be named and recognised explicitly, with Youthpass connected to Europass.

A higher bar for partnerships

One change will be felt in every round of partnership applications. To be funded, a proposal now needs 70% of the points available for relevance, instead of roughly half. New criteria reward projects “initiated, developed, and carried out by organisations actively involved in the field” and partnerships with “demonstrated experience” (p.240–241, p.249).

The aim is quality, and organisations rooted in their field should benefit. The risk is for newcomers and small grassroots groups, the organisations Small-scale Partnerships were designed to bring in. How evaluators apply the new criteria in the first round, on 9 March, will tell.

The transition is already being prepared

Several parts of the Guide build the bridge to the next programme before the co-legislators have agreed on it:

  • Reaccreditation. Organisations already accredited in any field can apply by 30 September 2027 to keep their accreditation in the next programme, valid until 31 December 2034 (p.44 to p.49). It is described as the first stage of a two-stage procedure, the second to be launched under the 2028–2034 Regulation, and the Commission reserves the right to modify or cancel the call.
  • No new youth accreditation. There is no call for first-time youth accreditations in 2027, so organisations that are not accredited have no new route until the next programme.
  • European School Alliances. European Partnerships for School Development are refocused on basic skills as the forerunner of the “European School Alliances” proposed for 2028–2034, “pending the approval of the legal base by the European legislators” (p.252).
  • The 2029 elections. European Youth Together now states that the 2029 European Parliament elections are relevant to the action (p.375).

The Guide is transparent that all of this depends on the final Regulation. That is precisely why 2027 matters: the shape of the next programme is being decided now, in the negotiations between the European Parliament and the Member States.

What it means for the Paper

The 2027 Guide is not the next programme. But it shows the direction of travel: skills and basic competences at the centre, rates frozen in the final year, the Solidarity Corps on its way into Erasmus+, and a first step on recognition. On some points it moves towards what the Paper asks for, on others away from it. We will keep reading each new text against the four demands, and keep sharing the Paper with those who will decide the next programme.

How we did this. We compared version 1 of the 2027 Guide (18 November 2026, 466 pages) with version 1 of the 2026 Guide (12 November 2025, 456 pages). Page numbers refer to the 2027 Guide. Corrections to the 2027 Guide are likely in the coming months; we will update this analysis if they change any of the points above.
